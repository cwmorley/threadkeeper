# Editorial assessment: The Murder of Roger Ackroyd
## What the writer should do
The central mystery is built around a distinction between what happened and what the narrator chose to report. A revision that makes Dr. Sheppard innocent must replace that mechanism's final explanation, not just soften his characterization.

The test request—make Sheppard innocent while preserving every event, clue, alibi, and the final reveal—is internally incompatible. Chapter XXVII explicitly admits his planning, murder, staging, and concealment. Preserve the experience of a surprising retrospective reveal if desired; the content of that reveal must change.

Recommended revision brief for this demonstration: **Sheppard did not commit the murder, but concealed Ralph's whereabouts through misguided loyalty. Preserve the recorded-voice puzzle and the independent secrets of the supporting cast where a replacement explanation can support them. Permit changes to the telephone call, evidence handling, and ending.**

This is a proposed creative direction, not an accepted change to the novel. The replacement murderer and method are still design decisions. No complete alternative solution has been proved here.

## What works and should survive
**The clue chain links several otherwise ordinary details.** In Chapter XXV, the telephone call ensures discovery that evening; the moved chair hides a table; the missing dictaphone requires a means of removal; the doctor's bag supplies that means. Chapter XXVI adds the unexplained extra travel time and the apparently corroborated call. These clues reinforce one another instead of each independently announcing the solution. Preserve this degree of interdependence in a new ending. Evidence: source lines 10131–10405. Confidence: high.

**The supporting cast's secrets have separate causes.** Flora's false visit conceals theft, while Miss Russell's concealment protects her son. Those motives explain misleading testimony without making everyone a murderer. Keep these distinctions: merging all suspicious behavior into the new killer's plot would flatten the novel. Evidence: Chapter XIX, lines 8195–8283; Chapter XX, lines 8650–8766. Confidence: high.

**Ordinary narration becomes evidence on rereading.** The vague description of leaving Ackroyd in Chapter IV and of attending the body in Chapter V acquires a specific meaning in Chapter XXVII. That is purposeful withholding within this novel's design. An editor should identify its function before recommending that it be made more explicit. Evidence: lines 1652–1657, 1867–1870, 10484–10512. Confidence: high.

## A real continuity finding: evolving testimony is not automatically a contradiction
Flora initially claims a late visit to her uncle. In XIX she admits the claim concealed theft and says she did not see him after dinner. In XX Raglan explicitly recognizes that the alibis must be reconsidered.

Editorial disposition: **intentional correction of testimony, adequately acknowledged in the passages examined. Do not “fix” the two accounts into agreement.** The useful task is to check whether subsequent reasoning uses the corrected account.

A second instance appears in XXIII and XXV. Hearing Ackroyd's voice is initially treated as proof that he was alive; the playback explanation removes that inference while retaining the audible event. Preserve the difference between an observation and a conclusion drawn from it.

## Concrete rewrite example
Target: Chapter V, around source line 1867. Current passage:

> I did what little had to be done.

The original uses this sentence to conceal evidence removal. Below is an original demonstration of an innocent narrator treating the body. It is a proposal, not an applied edit or a complete alternative plot.

> Parker hurried away. I took Roger's wrist, though the answer was already plain, and set it down precisely where I had found it. The dagger must remain untouched.
>
> I put my bag on the floor beside the desk. Its catch resisted me; for a moment I stared at the ridiculous little obstruction, grateful for a problem that admitted of being solved.
>
> Raymond appeared before I had opened it.
>
> “Is there nothing—?”
>
> I shook my head.
>
> He looked at Roger, then at me. I remember wishing he would say something more. Anything would have been better than standing there with the bag between my feet and no use for what it contained.

What this changes: it replaces an unexplained action with clinical observation, retains restraint, and gives the closed bag an observable role when Raymond arrives. The narrator's account alone still does not prove innocence. If the bag detail becomes exculpatory evidence, corroborate it independently.

What it breaks downstream: XXVII can no longer truthfully explain this interval as the doctor's removal of the dictaphone. The revised killer needs another opportunity. This example deliberately exposes that dependency instead of concealing it behind polished prose.

Voice assessment: the draft retains first-person observation and controlled emotion, but is more emotionally explicit than the source paragraph. Decide whether that greater emotional access is a deliberate new characterization before applying it throughout the book.
